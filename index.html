    What the research says

    How solid the evidence is for each variable, and how the model uses it.

    Load Strong evidence

    Reviews agree that activation of the prime movers climbs as the bar gets heavier, and that load changes more than stance width, hip rotation or switching to a front squat does.

    Model: every lever is multiplied by bar weight plus bodyweight above the knees.

    Sources: Clark 2012 review, PLOS One 2026

    Depth Mixed evidence

    Partial to parallel raises quad and glute activity. Parallel and full depth look alike on EMG when each is loaded to its own max. Yet ten weeks of full squats grew glutes and adductors two to three times as much as half squats, with similar quad growth and no hamstring growth. Below parallel, EMG and growth disagree.

    Model: takes the peak demand anywhere in the rep, so deeper is never less. The knee lever keeps growing below parallel; the hip lever peaks near parallel.

    Sources: Hammond 2016, Contreras 2016, Kubo 2019, Bryanton thesis

    Stance width Moderate evidence

    Wider stances raise glute max activity, by 13 to 61 percent across studies. Quads, hamstrings and glute medius barely change, the calf drops slightly, and overall adductor activity stays about the same on surface EMG. One modelling study found narrow stances raise quad forces and wide stances raise posterior-chain forces.

    Model: a wider stance turns the thighs out, shortens their reach front to back, and shifts hip work toward the glutes. The adductor increase it shows is an estimate, since surface EMG reads adductor magnus poorly.

    Sources: Straub & Powers 2024 review, Paoli 2009, Sinclair 2022

    Toe angle Moderate evidence, small effect

    Turning the feet out up to 30 degrees does not change quad, hamstring or calf activity. Adductor activity rose with 30 and 50 degrees of turnout in a study the review cites. Toe angle matters more for comfort and knee tracking than for targeting a muscle.

    Model: a small adductor effect, a small change in lean, and a warning when feet and thighs point in different directions.

    Sources: Straub & Powers 2024 review, Clark 2012 review

    Torso lean Mostly mechanics

    Leaning forward sends the hips back, which lengthens the lever at the hips and lower back and shortens it at the knees. A biomechanics review treats trunk and shin angle as what sets the hip-to-knee split, and blames uncontrolled trunk angle for the conflicting stance-width results.

    Model: the bar must stay over midfoot, so lean and knee travel trade off directly. This is the single strongest lever in the tool after load.

    Sources: Straub & Powers 2024 review

    Bar position Moderate evidence

    Low bar goes with more forward lean and more erector, adductor and glute activity; high bar with more quad activity. With load, depth and stance matched, though, low bar showed lower erector and quad activity, so much of its effect comes from the lean and the heavier weights it allows. A direct comparison of front and back squats found no significant EMG differences.

    Model: bar position sets where the load sits on the torso, which sets the lean needed for balance. A front rack forces an upright torso and loads the upper back.

    Sources: Glassbrook 2017 review, van den Tillaar 2020, Gullett 2009

    Heel elevation Mixed evidence

    Several studies find heel wedges and lifting shoes increase knee bend and, in some conditions, give a more upright trunk. One study of recreational lifters found no change in trunk angle or in quad and back muscle activity.

    Model: a raised heel adds ankle range, which lets the knees travel forward. The effect is small unless your ankles are the limit.

    Sources: heel wedge study, Legg 2017, Lee 2019

    Body proportions Mostly mechanics

    Longer femurs relative to the torso force more forward lean to keep the bar over midfoot. That is geometry. Direct muscle comparisons between builds are scarce.

    Model: femur and torso length change the levers directly; ankle mobility limits how far the knees can travel.

    Sources: Contreras, femur length and squat mechanics

    Which muscles work hardest Systematic review

    Across deadlift studies, the spinal erectors and quadriceps are more active than the glute max and biceps femoris, and semitendinosus is slightly more active than biceps femoris. The review also warns that methods differ so much between studies that numbers are hard to compare.

    Model: the long lever at the hips and lower back drives the erectors, glutes and hamstrings. The quads stay busy even with a short knee lever, because they have to cancel the pull of the hamstrings at the knee.

    Sources: Martín-Fuentes 2020 review

    Conventional or sumo Good evidence

    Sumo keeps the trunk 5 to 10 degrees more upright and the thighs more horizontal, and conventional lifters moved the bar 25 to 40 percent farther. Sumo raises quad activity and knee demand (a knee moment of about 80 against 53 N·m at 85% load in one study) and adds a large sideways demand at the hip. Hip extension demand is similar or slightly lower. Conventional showed more biceps femoris and calf activity.

    Model: a wide stance turns the thighs out, which brings the hips closer to the bar and shortens the lever at the lower back. The adductor increase it shows follows the sideways hip demand and is an estimate.

    Sources: Escamilla 2000, Escamilla 2002, Hanen 2025

    Straight bar or trap bar Good evidence

    A trap bar gives lower peak moments at the lower back, hips and ankles and a higher peak moment at the knees. EMG agrees: more vastus lateralis with the trap bar, more biceps femoris and erector activity with the straight bar. Powerlifters pulled about 8 percent more with a trap bar in one study; another found no difference in max.

    Model: with no bar in front of the shins, the knees travel forward and the hips sit closer to the load. High handles shorten the pull.

    Sources: Swinton 2011, Camara 2016

    Hip height, Romanian and stiff-leg Moderate evidence

    In maximal pulls against a fixed bar, a set-up with the bar closer and the hips higher gave more biceps femoris and upper-lumbar erector activity and less vastus lateralis, with a knee moment of 22 against 51 N·m and no difference at the hips or lower back. At the same bar weight, a Romanian deadlift showed far less rectus femoris activity than a conventional one (25 against 59 percent of peak) and slightly less glute max, and the net knee torque flipped from extensor to flexor.

    Model: less knee bend puts the knees behind the bar, so quad demand falls toward zero and the hamstrings and erectors take more. A review found erectors below hamstrings in the Romanian deadlift; the model keeps erectors on top at equal bar weight, so treat that ranking with caution.

    Sources: Edington 2018, Lee 2018, Martín-Fuentes 2020 review

    Back rounding and the Jefferson curl One study

    One EMG study compared the Jefferson curl with a Romanian deadlift at the same bar weight, about 40% of the lifters' deadlift max. The curl gave more lower-erector activity (60 against 48 percent of max), more upper-erector activity (63 against 45) and more biceps femoris (54 against 42), with no difference in glute max. Its authors note that current evidence does not clearly tie spinal flexion to back pain in healthy lifters. In maximal static pulls, a separate study found a fully flexed spine produced its back-extension moment with far less erector EMG than an arched one.

    Model: rounding shortens the trunk and lets the legs straighten, and curling and uncurling the spine adds erector and hamstring work in line with that study. It does not model a heavy deadlift held in a fixed rounded position, and it takes no position on safety.

    Sources: Yona 2026, Mawston 2021, summarised

    Start height Mechanics only

    No direct EMG comparison of deficit and block pulls turned up in the research for this tool, so this variable rests on geometry alone.

    Model: a lower start means more knee and hip bend, a longer knee lever and more bar travel. A higher start shortens the levers and the travel, most of all once the bar begins above the knee.

    Load Carried over from the squat

    Not checked separately for the deadlift. In the squat research, heavier loads raised activation across the prime movers and mattered more than any stance change.

    Model: every lever is multiplied by bar weight plus bodyweight; traps and forearms follow bar weight alone.

    Sources: Clark 2012 review

    Upper back, lats and grip Model estimate

    No EMG sources were checked for these. The bar hangs from the hands, so the traps and forearms should scale with bar weight in every variation.

    Model: the lat estimate depends on how far the shoulders sit ahead of the bar, which is largest with a conventional stance and near zero with a trap bar.

    Body proportions Mostly mechanics

    Arm length sets how high the shoulders start. Longer arms raise the hips and make the back more upright; long femurs do the opposite. This is geometry, with no EMG behind it.

    Model: femur, torso and arm length change the levers directly. Ankle mobility only matters with a trap bar, where the knees are free to travel.

    Load Strong evidence

    A systematic review calls intensity the most important factor in bench press EMG. In one study pec activity rose from 21 to 52 to 81 percent of max as the load went from 20 to 50 to 80 percent of one-rep max, and triceps from 31 to 55 to 80. Pecs and triceps were similarly active, both above the front delt.

    Model: every muscle scales with effort, which is the bar weight divided by what the chosen angle and grip let you lift.

    Sources: Stastny 2017 review

    Bench angle Good evidence

    At equal effort, the upper pec peaks at about 30 degrees of incline, the mid and lower pec are highest on a flat bench and fall as it tilts up, and the front delt climbs steadily with the angle. Triceps results conflict: unchanged in one study, far lower on the incline in another, which also found biceps about 50 to 70 percent higher there. Maxes drop about 22 percent at 30 degrees and 29 percent at 45. Older work found the upper pec least active on a decline.

    Model: a muscle-by-muscle pattern for each angle from those studies, with triceps set between the two conflicting results. At the same bar weight an incline is a harder effort, so everything rises.

    Sources: Rodríguez-Ridao 2020, Saeterbakken 2017, Sport Journal review

    Grip width Good evidence, small effect

    Across close, medium and wide grips at a six-rep max, pec activity did not differ. Biceps roughly doubled from close to wide (13, 19 and 25 percent of max), triceps eased off with the wide grip (70, 69 and 63), and the front delt was slightly lower with a close grip. Lifters handled about 7 percent less with the close grip. Older studies found a little more mid-chest with a wide grip and more upper chest and triceps with a close one.

    Model: those biceps, triceps and delt shifts, plus small pec shifts that split the difference between the newer and older studies.

    Sources: Saeterbakken 2021, Sport Journal review

    Back arch One study

    Benching with a flat back moved the bar 13 to 15 cm farther than an arched back in trained lifters, with no difference in pec activity for either head, a non-significant trend toward more triceps with the arch, and a slightly higher max with the arch.

    Model: the arch raises the chest, which shortens bar travel. Muscle levels barely move, apart from the back and legs holding the arch.

    Sources: Bartolomei 2024

    Elbow flare and touch point Mostly mechanics

    These follow from the grip. One study linked a grip of twice shoulder width or more to the upper arms more than 75 degrees out, and a grip under one and a half times shoulder width to less than 45 degrees.

    Model: forearms stay stacked under the bar, so a wider grip flares the elbows and moves the touch point up the chest. There is no separate elbow control, because no EMG study in this research isolated it.

    Sources: Sport Journal review

    Arm length Mechanics only

    Longer arms mean more bar travel. No EMG comparison by arm length was found.

    Model: travel comes from geometry, with a small assumed cost to how much you can lift.

    How the numbers are made

    Geometry first. The bar has to stay over the middle of the foot. Given your stance, bar, build and the other settings, the model works out where the knees and hips must sit. In the deadlift the arms hang straight to the bar, which fixes the shoulders and so the hips. The horizontal distance from each joint to the bar's line is its lever, and lever times load is the demand on that joint.

    Levers to muscles. The knee lever drives the quads. The hip lever is split between glutes, adductors and hamstrings according to hip angle and stance. The lean drives the spinal erectors. A standard squat (high bar, parallel, 80% load) is pinned to typical published EMG values, and every other squat and deadlift setup is scaled from it, so the two share one scale. That scale assumes your deadlift max is about 20% above your squat max. The bench press is built more directly from EMG studies: each muscle's level in a standard set, adjusted by the measured effects of angle, grip and effort.

    Your build. The sliders move one segment at a time. With measured lengths, each segment is compared with the average for that person's height, and that ratio is what changes the levers. Height alone does not move the muscle numbers, since a bigger frame carries bigger muscles. It does change three things: how low a standard plate or block sits against the body in the deadlift, how steep a given heel lift is, and the distances printed on the drawings. Average proportions follow the Drillis and Contini table used in most biomechanics texts, adjusted to points a tape can reach.

    Three patterns the map shows in every squat.

    • Hamstrings stay low. They cross the knee as well as the hip, so using them hard would fight the quads. Squat training did not grow them in Kubo 2019.
    • Adductors are a prime mover. Modelling suggests adductor magnus supplies half or more of the hip-extension torque in the squat (Vigotsky & Bryanton, summarised here).
    • Rectus femoris trails the other quads, because it also flexes the hip.

    Three patterns the map shows in every deadlift.

    • The lower back tops the chart. The hips start far behind the bar, and the erectors hold the trunk against that whole lever.
    • Hamstrings work here in a way they do not in the squat, because the knees stay near the bar and the hips do most of the moving.
    • Traps and forearms follow bar weight alone. No stance or bar choice changes what it takes to hang on.

    Three patterns the map shows in every bench press.

    • Pecs, front delts and triceps do nearly all the work, and they rise and fall together with effort.
    • Bench angle changes the mix more than anything else you can set: up the incline, the front delt and upper pec gain while the mid and lower pec fade.
    • Grip width and arch change the bar path much more than they change the muscles.

    Exercises built on the bench. Dumbbell presses, dips and flys start from studies that compared them with the barbell bench press at matched effort, so chest work sits on one scale. The dip adds lever geometry for lean and depth. Where no study covers a variable, the change comes from mechanics alone and the evidence note says so.

    Arm exercises. Triceps extensions take their two head values from one study that measured the long and lateral heads separately, read through the close-grip bench press that study shares with the bench model. Curls start from a standing curl and move with bar type, grip and exercise according to one study each. For both, the tension readouts are plain geometry: how far the forearm is from the line the weight pulls along. Research on arm exercises is thinner than on the big lifts, and each exercise says so at the top of the page.

    Back exercises. Pulldowns, pull-ups and rows start from one study that put the same lifters through six of them and recorded the lats, middle trapezius and lower-back erectors. Grip, bar path and elbow path then shift those numbers by what other studies report, damped where only one study exists. Biceps, rear delts and upper trapezius were not in that study, so their levels are estimates. The lat-stretch readout is geometry: how far the arm is raised from the side of the torso at the start of the pull.

    Search and filters. The picker at the top reads the model directly. An exercise counts as working a muscle when the model puts that muscle at 45 percent of its maximum or more in the exercise’s standard setup, and ranked lists use the same standard-setup numbers. Equipment is tagged by hand.

    Limits. Published EMG values for the same lift vary widely between studies, so trust the direction and rough size of a change more than any single number. EMG is not force and not growth. Muscles marked “est.” have little direct EMG data. Share of work is activation times approximate muscle size. Joint demand is lever times load, not tissue stress or injury risk. Nothing here is medical advice.
